Suppose you're looking for a lost scientific instrument. You search the same patch of ground, using the same sensor, five times. Nothing appears. How confident should you be that the instrument isn't there?

The answer depends not just on how good the sensor is, but on how it fails.

A deliberately small model

Let \(H\) mean that the instrument is present. We begin with \(P(H) = 1/2\). When the sensor works, it detects the instrument with probability \(d\). But there is a probability \(f\) that a persistent condition (the wrong soil, an acoustic shadow, a miscalibrated gain) makes it ineffective for every pass.

For simplicity, assume there are no false positives. The sensor's latent working state is fixed over the sequence, and detection attempts are independent conditional on that state.

If we incorrectly average over the sensor's working state anew on every pass, the likelihood of \(n\) misses given presence is

independent \[ L_\text{independent}(n) = \big(1 - (1-f)\,d\big)^n . \]

If instead the failure state persists across all passes,

shared failure \[ L_\text{shared}(n) = f + (1-f)(1-d)^n . \]

Since a missing instrument yields a miss with probability one, Bayes' rule with even prior odds gives

posterior \[ P(H \mid n \text{ misses}) = \frac{L(n)}{1 + L(n)} . \]

Both models agree before any searches and after the first one. After repeated misses they can disagree sharply: the shared-failure likelihood never falls below \(f\), however many times you look.

The fifth miss is not like the first

A run of misses can mean the instrument is absent. It can also mean the method isn't effective under these conditions. Once those alternatives are represented jointly, another failed attempt doesn't necessarily eliminate much probability: the history has itself become evidence about the sensor.

The immediate practical question is then no longer just where to search. It is whether the next observation should come from the same source at all. Push \(n\) to 12 in the lab: the same sensor's chance of finding the instrument on the next pass collapses, while a different sensor's barely moves.

This note became the seed of ABYSSAL's repeat-credit accounting and of its Search Health trap T6, repeating a failing method; Part 3 of the ABYSSAL series shows how the idea scales to overlapping searches on a map.

A direction for further research

The example above assumes we already know \(f\) and \(d\). In a real system they are often uncertain too. How much data would distinguish persistent unreliability from absence? What observation would best resolve the ambiguity? How badly do decisions suffer when we pretend the measurements are independent? Those questions lead from a toy Bayesian calculation towards experimental design, calibrated inference and the evaluation of intelligent agents.

This is a working explanatory note, not a novel research result. The underlying phenomenon is well known in Bayesian modelling of imperfect detection.
