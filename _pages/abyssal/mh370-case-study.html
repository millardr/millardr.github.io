TL;DR
  • Everything here is public data with receipts, and the case is an ordinary ABYSSAL project: no MH370 plugin, no special buttons. The satellite arc is a ring observation, the flight model is a distance ring with alternative readings, the debris and barnacles are area statements, and every seabed search is a completed search with a detection probability.
  • Satellite timing, Doppler and a calibrated Boeing 777 fuel model together put the end of the flight on the 7th arc between 33°S and 38.8°S, most likely 37–38.5°S. The far south (beyond 39.5°S) runs out of fuel; the north (beyond 32.5°S) cannot be reached by any manoeuvre that fits the data.
  • Coverage gaps cannot hide it. The public sonar mosaic images 99.67% of the ±25 NM band in the high-probability zone. If MH370 lies in searched ground, it was imaged and missed, most plausibly on steep fracture-zone walls where towed sonar casts shadows.
  • Hydrophones, seismometers, oil slicks, altimeters, contrails, radio propagation, ship tracks: all tried on real 2014 data, all null or impossible. Each is documented below with the number that decides it.
  • The final map puts about 43% of the probability in a 22,800 km² re-look of the fuel-peak core (36.8–38.8°S, within 15 NM of the arc), and 12% in a single 2,600 km² escarpment massif near 38.1°S, 88.2°E, the densest ground in the Indian Ocean on this evidence. Five of the ATSB's own 43 published sonar contacts lie inside it, all reviewed and none identified as wreckage.

Malaysia Airlines flight 370 left Kuala Lumpur at 00:42 local time on 8 March 2014 with 239 people on board and has never been found. Since then the Australian Transport Safety Bureau (ATSB) and Ocean Infinity have imaged more than 230,000 km² of the southern Indian Ocean floor. Dozens of analysts have argued over a handful of satellite messages, some drifting debris and a great deal of empty sea.

This page rebuilds the whole public investigation from raw data inside ABYSSAL, a probabilistic search workbench, and tells it in the order the evidence arrives. It starts from a blank map of 33 million km² and adds one piece of evidence at a time. Every engine gets a turn, including the ones that failed, and each step shows what the map looks like afterwards. Nothing here is a detection. It is an argument about where to look, laid out so that every number can be checked.

How this was built

The case is a new, ordinary ABYSSAL project. Every observation went in through the same public interface the app's Add and Sketch evidence panels use, as an explicit, logged decision. Context items (radar fixes, debris find places, sonar contacts) stay as drafts and never enter the model. The heavy physics (satellite geometry, flight paths through real winds, the fuel model, drift, acoustics) runs in ABYSSAL's general signal engines. The app only receives their outputs, in its own generic vocabulary. The screenshots are the real application, captured automatically from a running workbench. The only display tweak is hiding floating toolbars on the full-screen maps.

00The night, in two minutes

At 17:19:30 UTC the crew said “Good night Malaysian three seven zero” to Kuala Lumpur air traffic control. Seventy seconds later, over waypoint IGARI in the South China Sea, the transponder went dark. Malaysian military primary radar, which needs no cooperation from the aircraft, then watched a target turn back across the Malay Peninsula, pass south of Penang at 17:52 and head out over the Strait of Malacca. The last radar return came at 18:22:12, 10 NM beyond waypoint MEKAR, north-west of Sumatra.

Three minutes later the aircraft's satellite terminal logged back on to Inmarsat's 3F1 satellite over the Indian Ocean. For six more hours it answered routine hourly calls from the ground station in Perth. None of those messages carried a position. At 00:19:29 UTC the terminal logged on again, unprompted. Investigators concluded this was the auxiliary power unit restarting it after both engines ran out of fuel. Eight seconds later it sent one more burst, and then nothing. The full public chronology is retold, without analysis, in the MH370 essay.

01Starting from nothing

A search in ABYSSAL begins with an honest admission of ignorance. The new case covers the whole region the aircraft could conceivably have reached, from 50°S to 10°N and 70°E to 120°E. With no evidence every square kilometre is equally likely. The map is a flat wash, its 90% region is the entire box of 33,285,051 km², and its entropy is 16.25 bits.

Step 0 An empty case 90% region 33,285,051 km² · 0 observations

The first real step does not change the map at all. Each primary source goes into the case library with its link and a note on what it says: the Malaysian safety investigation, the Inmarsat paper, the ATSB search report, the drifter archive, the hydrophone archive, the sonar mosaic, the sea-temperature record and the published statements on the later searches. Retaining a source is not evidence. The map moves only when someone decides what a source means and admits that interpretation as an observation.

The investigation behind this case keeps the same discipline for its raw data. Every file it downloaded carries a receipt: the URL, retrieval time, size, SHA-256 hash and licence. That covers about 4 GB of reanalysis winds, seismograms, drifter tracks, hydrophone recordings, sonar tiles, satellite images and altimeter passes. 02Where it was is not where it ended

The last radar return, at 18:22:12 UTC, is a hard fact about where MH370 was. It is not evidence of where the flight ended six hours later, so it goes into the case as a draft, visible on the map and kept out of the model. In ABYSSAL a dashed outline means exactly that: recorded, not admitted.

03Seven handshakes: the satellite rings

Two numbers in each satellite message do all the work. The burst timing offset (BTO) is how late the reply arrived. It measures the round trip from Perth to the satellite, to the aircraft and back, so it fixes the aircraft's distance from the satellite. The burst frequency offset (BFO) is a small shift in the radio frequency, set mostly by the aircraft's speed towards or away from the satellite. On its own it cannot place the aircraft, but it tells you which way along the rings the aircraft was moving.

The investigation rebuilt both from first principles in abyssal.signal.satcom. It uses the published satellite ephemeris, the ground-station geometry, the terminal's Doppler pre-compensation and the satellite's own frequency drift. The model reproduces all twelve rows of Inmarsat's published frequency table (Ashton et al. 2015, Table 9) to within 1.5 Hz, and the ATSB's reference 7th arc.

One finding matters for what follows. Projected onto the Earth, the 7th arc is a geodesic circle of radius 4,924.5 km ± 2.5 km around the satellite's sub-point (0.535°N, 64.464°E). ABYSSAL's generic ring observation stays within 5.4 km of the reference arc across the search region, and within 6.8 km along its whole length, far inside the ring's own ±19 km width. A ring around a fixed point is exactly what an acoustic range, a radio range or a satellite timing gives, so no MH370-specific code is needed.

The ring needs a width, and that depends on how the flight ended. The last two BFOs, at 00:19:29 and 00:19:37, imply a descent of about 4,400 ft/min steepening to about 14,900 ft/min eight seconds later. That is a downward acceleration of roughly 0.68 g: an unpiloted, steepening dive after fuel exhaustion. A controlled glide would need a recovery from that dive. The investigation also checked the satellite signal strength recorded for those two bursts: 40.6 and 43.4 dB-Hz, with zero bit errors. The aircraft's antenna was still locked onto the satellite eight seconds into the descent, which rules out a near-vertical dive at that moment.

So the 7th arc goes into ABYSSAL as one ring with alternative readings. A dive lands within about 10 NM of the arc (95.2% weight). A recovered glide could carry up to 110 NM, 70% of it on the far side (4.8% weight after the final-BFO penalty). The readings are mutually exclusive interpretations inside one observation, not separate evidence, and ABYSSAL marginalises over them.

04Which way along the ring: Doppler

The ring runs from the Southern Ocean up through Indonesia. Inmarsat's original insight was that the frequency offsets fit a southbound aircraft and not a northbound one. For a northern route the BFO residuals run to tens of hertz, against a measurement noise of about 5–7 Hz. Military radar coverage and the lack of any northern-hemisphere sighting point the same way. In ABYSSAL this is an area statement: the region north of 15°S is a thousand times less likely than the rest.

Step 4 Doppler says south 90% region 620,279 km² · entropy 10.65 bits

05The flight engine: winds, autopilots and fuel

The ring says how far from the satellite. It does not say where along the ring, and that is where most analyses have disagreed. Answering it means flying the aircraft. The investigation built a general flight engine, abyssal.signal.flightpath, for this. It integrates candidate autopilot paths through real hourly ERA5 winds and temperatures, checked against 9M-MRO's own ACARS wind reports to within 3° and 2 kt, and scores each path against every satellite message.

After the last radar fix, each candidate flies along airway N571 and makes a single final major turn between 18:29 and 18:39. It then holds one of five autopilot modes (great circle, true or magnetic track, true or magnetic heading) at a constant or slowly falling Mach number and altitude. A Gauss–Newton fit and a Laplace evidence integral weight each configuration. Three families came out:

  • Family A, constant Mach: 1,100 configurations.
  • Family B, falling Mach, a stand-in for long-range cruise: 2,200 configurations.
  • Family C, slow speeds allowed with down-weighted Doppler, as a hedge against a wrong BFO model: 1,100 configurations.

All three end between 37.4°S and 39.7°S, with χ² of 4–6 for 13 measurements.

Then a sharper test. If the Doppler model were wrong, where could the aircraft be? A timing-only scan dropped the BFOs entirely and allowed speeds down to Mach 0.45. The satellite timing alone still puts 90% of the end points between 37.4°S and 39.7°S. Slow northern paths fit the timing rings about 1010 times worse. The best timing-only paths match the Doppler data to 4–5 Hz even though Doppler was never used to fit them. The two satellite channels agree independently.

A Boeing 777 that has to run out of fuel at 00:17

The families above say nothing about fuel, yet fuel is the hardest constraint in the problem: the aircraft had to run dry at about 00:17 UTC, not an hour earlier or later. Revision 3 of the investigation added a full aerodynamic fuel model, abyssal.signal.aircraft_fuel. It combines a Boeing 777-200ER drag polar, transonic wave-drag rise and Rolls-Royce Trent 892 fuel consumption scaled by temperature and Mach, and integrates the aircraft's falling weight through the real ERA5 temperatures. It was calibrated against the aircraft's own data before it was allowed to score anything:

  • The ACARS cruise at 17:01–17:06 (FL350, Mach 0.82, 218.3 t): modelled 6,955 kg/h against 6,967 ± 218 kg/h derived from the aircraft's reported weights, a 0.2% difference.
  • The planned Beijing trip fuel: about 36,700 kg modelled against 37,200 kg in the flight plan (−1.3%).
  • The planned endurance of 7 h 31 min: 49,150 kg modelled against 49,100 kg (+0.1%).

The budget follows. At 17:06:43 the aircraft held 43,800 kg, less 250 kg unusable. Flying the 597 NM military-radar leg through the real atmosphere burns 8,249 kg, leaving 35,301 kg at 18:22:12. Every candidate path must then burn exactly that by about 00:17. The result is decisive:

  • The far south closes. The probability south of 39.45°S falls from 3.9–8.9% to 0.00–0.02% in every family. It stays under 0.2% even with a 4% more efficient airframe or a fuel uncertainty of ±1,800 kg.
  • The altitude is pinned. FL300 runs dry about an hour early, while FL390–FL410 carries 86% of the weight.
  • The aircraft's BFO bias is pinned. Even when it is left free (±25 Hz), the data put it at −0.4 Hz.

What if nobody flew a straight line?

Steady autopilot flight after one turn is an assumption. Revision 3 tested it with 155,000 manoeuvring flights (abyssal.signal.nonsteady_flight). They include holding patterns of up to 70 minutes before 19:41, two-leg flights with mid-flight heading, speed or altitude changes, low-altitude cruise at high fuel burn, and the unpowered slowdown after flameout. Each was refined against the 6 BTOs, 7 BFOs and the fuel budget, in every 1° bin from 41°S to 18°S.

The answer is a cliff at both ends. South of 39°S no manoeuvre works: at 40°S the best path is 7,105 kg short of fuel. North of 32.5°S the timing and Doppler fits collapse, with χ² of 53–367 against about 6 timing points and BFO residuals of 15–21 Hz. In between, 33–38.5°S is flyable. The global best fit is at 37°S: a climb from FL392 to FL410 with the speed easing from Mach 0.84 to 0.79, timing χ² 0.3, Doppler 4.5 Hz, 170 kg to spare. A late-descent family reaches 34–33°S. The 10% "anything goes" allowance that the previous revision had spread along the arc is replaced by physics.

In ABYSSAL the whole flight engine becomes one more ring. It measures distance flown from the last radar fix, and its eight alternative readings are a Gaussian mixture fitted to the engine's end-point density: 80% fuel-coupled steady families, 20% manoeuvring scan. The fit reproduces the engine's distribution along the arc to within 1.7% total variation. Where this ring crosses the 7th arc, the map collapses.

06What the sea returned

On 29 July 2015 a flaperon washed up on Réunion. Over the next two years about 30 more pieces turned up in Mozambique, South Africa, Mauritius, Madagascar and Tanzania. The Malaysian report classes 27 of them as confirmed, probable or unverified. Their timing and places are evidence about where they started, if you can model how the sea carried them.

Debris drift, scored against real drifters

The investigation's drift engine, abyssal.signal.drift_markov, uses 6.0 million real drifting-buoy fixes from NOAA's Global Drifter Program, 1990–2025, as analogs. For each candidate start latitude it asks how often real drifters from there reached each find region by each find date. A Markov chain on a 1° grid was tried first and rejected, because it over-diffuses over one to two years. The direct analog count, with censoring for finds that might have arrived earlier unseen, is what is used.

The answer depends on windage, how much faster than a drogued drifter wind-blown debris travels, which is the biggest single uncertainty in the problem. If debris drifted like the buoys, the timing favours starts north of about 31°S. If it drifted 1.5 times faster, the arc is nearly flat.

In ABYSSAL the drift likelihood, averaged over the three windage stretches, goes in as thirteen relative-density bands along the arc. Each is an area statement of the form "this band is 0.24 times as likely as the most favoured band", sourced to the drifter analysis. A second set of five bands encodes a silence: nothing washed up in Western Australia. Real drifters from the far south often reach the WA coast within two years, and no MH370 debris was ever found there.

Step 6 Debris drift 90% region 94,437 km²

The barnacles' thermometer

The flaperon arrived covered in goose barnacles, Lepas anatifera, which only settle and grow in water warmer than roughly 18–20°C. Revision 3 pulled the real NOAA OISST v2.1 sea-surface temperatures for March–May 2014 along the arc. It first validated them off Réunion: the June 2015 OISST of 24.8°C matches the 25.4 ± 1.0°C measured from the barnacles' final growth. At 40°S the sea was 15.2–15.4°C, too cold even if the flaperon drifted 2.5° north within 60 days. At 34–36°S it was 18.3–19.9°C from the start.

07Looking at the surface

The 2014 air and sea search

From 18 March to 28 April 2014, aircraft and ships from several nations searched the surface for floating debris, moving north along the arc as the analysis changed. They found nothing, and that is evidence too. CSIRO computed the probability that the search would have seen low-windage debris for each impact latitude (ATSB 2017, Figure 20). It was near-certain for 26–32.5°S, where the search spent late March, and small in the far south. ABYSSAL takes it as eight bands of 1 − 0.8 × POD, where 0.8 is the assumed chance that recognisable debris was afloat to be seen.

Oil slicks, sun glint and the best day to look

A crash leaves a trace on the surface, even after fuel exhaustion. Unusable fuel, engine oil and hydraulic fluid amount to perhaps 0.1–1 m³. That can make a sheen of order 1–10 km², which drifts with the current plus about 3% of the wind and shows up as a bright or dark smooth patch in sun glint. The investigation drifted a sheen forward from every point on the arc using real March 2014 currents (OSCAR) and winds (NAVGEM). It then pulled NASA MODIS images from Terra and Aqua centred on where the sheen should have been at each overpass from 8 to 12 March.

It failed, for reasons worth knowing. Over the south extension the predicted sheen never had a clear look in five days: every pass was cloud. Clear looks exist at 41°S (Terra, 10 March) and 34°S (Aqua, 11 March), but outside sun glint. There the free true-colour product spans about one grey level of dark ocean: a standard deviation of 0.93 brightness steps out of 255. A sheen simply cannot be seen in it, even over a fully clear scene. A real attempt needs calibrated radiances and glint geometry.

A radar altimeter flew over the predicted sheen

An oil film damps the centimetre ripples that scatter radar. A nadir altimeter therefore sees a sharp jump in backscatter over a slick, called a "sigma-zero bloom". The investigation read every Jason-2 pass from 8 to 12 March 2014 (128 passes, 22 crossing the region) and interpolated each predicted sheen track to the exact time of each crossing. One coincidence stands out. Pass 077 flew straight over the predicted sheen of a 40.0°S impact, 11 hours after the crash, at 11:25 UTC on 8 March, with 7 one-second measurements inside the uncertainty circle. Its backscatter was flat: +0.05 dB at a wind of 6.9 m/s, so no bloom. A sheen a few kilometres wide inside a 32 km circle would be hit only 10–15% of the time, so this is weak evidence against the far south, consistent with the fuel result.

Radar ripple rings and dark vessels

Synthetic-aperture radar would show slicks, ship wakes and the ring of ripples from an impact regardless of cloud. There was none to look at. ENVISAT ended in 2012, and Sentinel-1A launched on 3 April 2014, four weeks too late. Commercial archives from RADARSAT-2, TerraSAR-X and COSMO-SkyMed are paid. Historical satellite AIS ship tracks from 2014 are commercial or need a registered key. ABYSSAL's Signal Lab has the engines (Sentinel-1 scene search, SAR vessel detection, dark-track matching), but for March 2014 the honest result is no data. The objects that satellites photographed near 44–45°S in mid-March 2014 do not fit either. The nearest point on the arc is 732 km from the Chinese Gaofen-1 object, which would need 0.85 m/s of sustained drift for ten days.

These are the matching engines in ABYSSAL's Signal Lab, open on this case. The investigation ran the acoustic and seismic ones on real 2014 recordings (next chapter). The radar, vessel and ripple-ring engines have no March 2014 input at all. In the app, each panel runs only on its own labelled SCENARIO demo until real data is loaded, and nothing from them entered this case.

08Listening for the impact

A 200-tonne aircraft hitting the sea at several hundred kilometres an hour makes sound, and the deep sound channel, the SOFAR channel, carries sound thousands of kilometres. Even a weak signal, if recorded, could be matched across instruments by its arrival times. This was the most thoroughly tried idea in the investigation, and the most thoroughly negative.

Hydrophones that were recording

IMOS publishes raw recordings from its passive acoustic observatories. The MH370 release covers two loggers in the Perth Canyon and one at Scott Reef. Each records 5 minutes in every 15 at 6 kHz, and the two Perth Canyon loggers are interleaved. The investigation reduced every recording from 7 to 8 March to per-second band energies. A dive impact anywhere on most of the arc would have arrived while a Perth Canyon logger was recording, and they heard nothing unusual. Only a blind band near 32.5–34.5°S fell into the duty-cycle gaps.

It then found a hydrophone nobody had used. IMOS deployment 3274 off Portland, Victoria, recording since 30 December 2013, was running all night. Its 01:14:46–01:19:53 recording covers exactly when sound from an impact at 39.0–41.0°S would have arrived, the south extension again. The recording is quiet. Portland's band is busy, though: short 10–30 Hz bursts every few minutes, whale calls, ice, distant earthquakes. A catalogued magnitude-4.0 ridge earthquake 2,400 km away does not stand out from that clutter.

The most sensitive test adds the faint energy at all four loggers together, at the arrival times predicted for every candidate impact point and time. Paths that cross land are excluded. The null is the same statistic shifted by whole 15-minute periods, which preserves the recording on/off pattern exactly. No latitude stands out: the lowest p-value among 97 latitudes is 0.047, about what chance gives. The south extension's windows are, if anything, quieter than usual.

Revision 3 then screened every path through 3-D ETOPO 2022 bathymetry. Broken Ridge, near 32°S, rises to 780–870 m below the surface and blocks the deep sound channel to Perth Canyon, Portland and the CTBTO station HA01 (transmission 0.18–0.27). Paths from 34–42°S stay deeper than 2,090 m and are unobstructed. The hydrophone factor that goes into ABYSSAL (eleven bands) uses the Perth Canyon coverage, screened by that blocking, with an assumed detection probability of 0.5. That probability is uncalibrated, because nobody knows how loud an airliner impact is at 4,000 km.

Implosions, engine tones and the 01:34 signal

Air trapped in sinking wreckage collapses at depth with a bubble-pulse signature, the way the submarine ARA San Juan was heard 6,000 km away. The investigation catalogued every impulsive transient in the IMOS recordings from 00:00 to 03:30 UTC (252 events) and tested each for an implosion bubble train with ABYSSAL's cepstral detector. None matched the arrival window of an implosion on the arc. It also re-examined the signal Curtin University reported near 01:34 UTC. It is present in the Perth Canyon recording with no bubble train, and its published bearing points to the Carlsberg Ridge, far from the arc. Listening for the engines' tones in a tight band was a non-starter: no hydrophone lay within 1,000 km of the southern route, and almost none of the sound of an aircraft at 35,000 ft gets into the water.

Island seismometers, calibrated

Twenty-two island and coastal seismometers were recording, from Cocos, Christmas Island, Diego Garcia and the Maldives to Réunion, Amsterdam Island and Kerguelen. The investigation built a T-phase back-projection, abyssal.signal.tphase, with an empirical time-shift null. Before letting it say anything about MH370, it calibrated it on real earthquakes from the same 72 hours. It detects a magnitude-5.1 Carlsberg Ridge earthquake but misses magnitude 3.7–4.0 ocean quakes at 300–3,200 km. An aircraft impact is far weaker, so the seismometers cannot hear it and their factor is flat. Two apparent coincidences turned out to be uncatalogued local earthquakes.

Seismometers under the known flight path

If seismometers cannot hear the impact, could they hear the aircraft itself? Aircraft show up on seismometers as Doppler-gliding tones, and MH370 passed almost directly over the Kulim seismometer (MY.KUM) at about 17:49–17:50 UTC on its turn back across the peninsula. The open data, though, are sampled at 20 per second, which cuts off everything above 10 Hz, where most jet noise lives. Nothing matches the overflight timing, and the features that look aircraft-like at Ipoh recur on the control night. The station 22 km from the 16:46 climb-out position has no public 2014 data.

Radio propagation, contrails and witnesses

Three more ideas were tested.

  • WSPR. Amateur radio propagation logs have been proposed as a tripwire for aircraft. The investigation tested the claim with a time-shift null over 30 March nights with the same geometry. The real night is unremarkable (p = 0.19–0.48) for both a 38°S and a 34°S track.
  • Contrails. A contrail on the way south would be a straight cold line in infrared imagery. GridSat-B1 is too coarse at 8 km. MODIS Aqua's night pass at about 19:20 UTC, 40 minutes after the final turn, shows many thin cirrus streaks on 7 March and on the control nights, and none can be tied to MH370.
  • The Maldives sighting. The island lies about 2,000 km nearer the satellite than the 7th arc, a timing residual of −13,200 µs against noise of 29–62 µs, and the reported time was an hour after the last log-on.

09Where people already looked

The seabed searches are the most powerful evidence in the case, and the easiest to get wrong. Each one is negative evidence: if the wreck had been here, it would probably have been seen. Its weight is the probability of detection (POD) over the ground actually covered. The published text describes the searched area only roughly, so the investigation measured it. It fetched the public Geoscience Australia Phase 2 sonar mosaic tile by tile and counted imaged pixels: 120,522 km², consistent with the ATSB's "120,000 km²".

Measuring corrected two errors. North of about 35.5°S, the ATSB's ±25 NM band was only 42–69% imaged. That is exactly the gap Ocean Infinity searched first in 2018, which the earlier text-based model had double-credited. In the high-probability band, the mosaic images 99.67% of ±25 NM. Gaps large enough to hide a 250 m debris field hold only 0.013% of the prior. MH370 is not hiding in a coverage gap.

Revision 3 then asked a harder question: imaged is not seen. Towed side-scan sonar casts acoustic shadows behind steep relief. ETOPO 2022 slopes inside the search band reach 18.6°, and two fracture-zone escarpments sit right on the fuel-constrained peak. In the real mosaic tiles over them, deep shadow covers 5.9% and 8.8% of the ground, 1.6 and 2.4 times the flat-seabed control, with dense hairpin turns of the towfish.

So ABYSSAL receives the ATSB search as four completed searches by terrain class:

  • POD 0.95 over flat seabed.
  • POD 0.90 over moderate slopes.
  • POD 0.84 over steep escarpment flanks.
  • POD 0.60 over the steepest, shadowed walls.

Three more completed searches follow:

  • Ocean Shield's AUV survey around the 2014 pinger detections near 21°S.
  • Ocean Infinity in 2018, credited only where the ATSB mosaic has no data.
  • Ocean Infinity in 2025–26, whose 7,571 km² of unpublished polygons are spread as a 25% effective coverage.

Twelve years in one slider

ABYSSAL's Retrospective replay filters the admitted ledger by event time and recomputes the map, so the searches appear in the order they were done: Ocean Shield in April 2014, the ATSB from October 2014 to January 2017, Ocean Infinity in 2018 and again in 2025–26. It uses the current evidence, filtered by date. It is not a reconstruction of what analysts believed at the time.

Last come the things that are true but are not evidence about the ending: the 27 debris find places, the 43 sonar contacts the ATSB flagged for review, the hydrophone and seismometer stations. They go in as context drafts. They appear on the map as references and never touch the belief.

10Reading the seabed again

If the wreck is in searched ground, it was imaged and missed. So the investigation re-read the public sonar mosaic over the densest searched ground with ABYSSAL's own anomaly detector, abyssal.detect.anomaly. It covered 2,024 tiles at about 3.8 m per pixel over 1,900 km² (25% of the steady-flight prior) and, in Revision 3, 1,452 more tiles over the northern fuel peak. The detector looks for clusters of bright returns that stand out from their own surroundings, which is what a field of wreckage looks like at this resolution.

It was calibrated first on the 43 contacts the original search flagged for review, including the two 19th-century shipwreck debris fields it found. It flags 16 of the 43 (37%) and puts the median contact in the top 12% of its window: real sensitivity, but far from perfect. The top 40 candidates were then inspected by eye at the finest zoom the tile server offers. All are geology: scarps, ridge crests, outcrops, boulders and swath seams. No debris field was recognised. The ATSB's analysts had the raw sonar at about 1 m; the public product is about 5 m.

The contacts on the massif

The published contact list is where the original search and this analysis meet. Nine of the 43 contacts lie within the frame below, and five lie inside the ~2,600 km² escarpment massif that the evidence now ranks first. That massif is about 2% of the ATSB's searched area but holds 12% of its contacts. Rough ground makes bright returns, so that is expected, and the reviewers' own notes say so. It also means this is the ground where a wreck would be hardest to tell apart from rock.

Nothing here is a detection. Every one of these contacts was reviewed by people with the full-resolution data, and none was identified as aircraft wreckage. Two records were sent for ROV dives and are classed as geology; the record for contact 8 notes that two of its three dives were aborted. Contact 7 was missed on the first side-scan pass and only picked up after a multibeam feature drew attention to it. That is a small, real illustration of the shadow problem on this terrain. The case for re-surveying the massif does not rest on any single contact. It rests on the fuel-constrained peak, the steep relief and the weaker sonar coverage there, and these records are the first things a re-survey should look at again.

For comparison with the published hotspots: the nearest contact to CSIRO's 2017 drift estimate (35.6°S 92.8°E) is 21 km away (“possible series of scattered items”), and the 2021 UGIB position is 73 km from the nearest contact, outside the searched band.

11Fusion: how the pieces combine

Every step above is one factor in a single Bayesian update, computed in log space. Each observation carries a reliability, the chance it is simply wrong, so one bad input cannot zero out the map. Observations that share a source root are pooled rather than multiplied. Completed searches by one method are pooled by strongest coverage per cell. The area statements are applied as likelihood factors, so their order does not matter.

ABYSSAL's Evidence Map lists what moves the belief, with one-click Remove and Undo for every item. The Provenance graph wires every observation back to the sources it came from. Under the current model version the per-item information figures for area statements read "Not assessed", because ABYSSAL only scores the classic observation kinds item by item. The map itself uses everything.

12The final map

Put together, the evidence leaves MH370 in one of two places. Either it is already inside searched ground, imaged and missed, most plausibly on the steep fracture-zone walls of the fuel-constrained peak. Or it is beyond the searched width after a controlled glide, which the final BFOs argue against. The far south and the north, the two frontiers that dominated the previous revision, are physically closed.

How this compares with the published analyses

  • DSTG (Davey et al. 2016) put the peak of its satellite-only distribution near 38°S. This analysis agrees on the satellite data: two satellite channels, flown through real winds with independent code, end in the same place. Fuel then moves the centre slightly north, to 37.8°S.
  • CSIRO's 2017 drift work favoured about 35.6°S, 92.8°E. Here the drift evidence also leans north, but only weakly once windage uncertainty is honest. It enters as one factor among many, not as a location.
  • The 2021 UGIB estimate (33.18°S, 95.30°E) sits in the 33–34°S late-descent corner that the manoeuvring scan finds flyable but unlikely. On this evidence the region carries 1–2%.

13Where to look next

The single densest target on this evidence is a compact 1,045 m submarine escarpment massif, about 2,600 km² around 38.1°S, 88.2°E. It sits directly on the fuel-constrained peak of the 7th arc and carries 12.1% of the whole posterior, about 4.6% per 1,000 km², 25 times denser than the outer glide belt. The real mosaic over it shows deep shadow and tangled towfish turns.

ABYSSAL's planning tools, Where next? and Plan Studio, run on the same map. Both are generic: they know nothing about MH370, only about the belief map, the searches already done and an asset's assumed sweep width and detection probability.

Recommendation

A multi-aspect synthetic-aperture sonar AUV re-survey, flying orthogonal headings over the ~2,600 km² escarpment massif at 37.7–38.25°S, 88.0–88.6°E, then the top 5,000–15,000 km² of steep terrain between 36.8°S and 38.6°S. On this evidence that ground is 10–30 times more likely per square kilometre than any unsearched ground. Before that, the cheapest decisive step is to obtain the raw sub-metre ATSB and Ocean Infinity shadow and low-confidence polygons over the massif, which would show whether the ground was truly cleared.

16Limits, and what would settle it

The final map is conditional on stated, swept assumptions. Revision 3 ran 17 sensitivity variants and 6 fuel-calibration variants, and the fuel-peak core ranks first in every one. That does not make it a measurement. The main limits are these:

  • The fuel model is a calibrated physical model, not Boeing's proprietary performance tables.
  • The manoeuvring scan allows at most two legs plus a hold.
  • Debris windage is uncertain by a factor of 1.5.
  • The hydrophone detection probability is uncalibrated.
  • The searched area is the published mosaic. Ocean Infinity's polygons are not public.
  • The 7th arc's dive-versus-glide split rests on an interpretation of two BFO values.
  • The observations in this ABYSSAL case were admitted by the author, as the case's analyst. In a real search each would be reviewed and admitted by a person accountable for it.

Four pieces of data would settle most of what remains:

  1. The raw ATSB, Fugro and Ocean Infinity sonar and low-confidence polygons over 36.8–38.8°S, especially the escarpments at 37.7–38.25°S.
  2. A warm-reboot bench test of the Honeywell satellite data unit's oscillator, to rule out a non-descent explanation of the final BFO. That decides the glide weight.
  3. CTBTO HA01 and HA08 recordings for 8 March 2014, which would turn a weak acoustic factor into a strong one.
  4. Isotope sclerochronology of the oldest flaperon barnacles, which would fix the water temperature at the start of the drift.

17Method and reproducibility

All code is in the ABYSSAL repository:

  • General engines in abyssal/signal/: satcom, upper_air, flightpath, aircraft_fuel, nonsteady_flight, tphase, drift_markov, corridor_fusion, sofar_blocking and bubble_pulse; plus abyssal/detect/anomaly.
  • Case drivers and results in cases/reference/mh370/investigation/ and data/mh370/investigation/.
  • The ABYSSAL case itself is built by abyssal_case_plan.py and abyssal_case_build.py, through the public HTTP API only. Anyone running the workbench can rebuild it and get the same map.

Every download has a receipt (URL, time, size, SHA-256, licence), and every result records its model version, code commit and input hashes. Nothing here has been admitted to any reference case.
