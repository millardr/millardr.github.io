The Fifth
Miss.

When does accumulating more evidence make you more certain without making you more right?

RORY MILLARD·ABYSSAL RESEARCH·ILLUSTRATIVE PROBABILITY MODEL

Imagine you've lost a scientific instrument in a survey area. You have a sensor capable of detecting it, but after five searches you still haven't found it. How confident should you be that the instrument isn't there?

That depends not just on the sensor's average accuracy, but on whether its failures can persist from one observation to the next.

If a sensor can enter a hidden, persistent failure state, repeated misses may tell us more about the sensor than about the instrument's location.

One observation, two models

Assume the object has a 50% prior probability of being present. When working, the sensor detects it with probability d. Before the search begins, there is probability f that the sensor is completely ineffective for the entire series of observations.

The two models agree on the average probability of a miss on the first pass. They disagree about what repeated misses mean.

Lindependent(n) = [1 − (1 − f)d]n
Lshared(n) = f + (1 − f)(1 − d)n

Each L(n) is the likelihood of n consecutive misses if the object is present. We assume no false positives, so the likelihood of all misses when the object is absent is one.

With a 50% prior, the posterior that the object is present after those misses is simply L(n) / [1 + L(n)].

The unsettling part

The evidence hasn't changed. Only our model of the observation process has. Both models are coherent under their own assumptions, but they make different predictions about the value of searching again.

In the shared-failure model, a fifth unsuccessful pass can actually increase our suspicion that the sensor is ineffective. Rather than simply asking Where should we look next?, we may need to ask What would give us genuinely independent information?

The absence of a signal is evidence only to the extent that we trust the instrument that failed to produce it.

What this doesn't prove

This toy model does not estimate real-world failure rates, and accounting for dependence is not always better when the dependence model is itself wrong. It illustrates why independence assumptions matter. More general models, empirical calibration, and decision policies are subjects for future research notes.

Where this leads

The wider ABYSSAL research programme asks how systems should reason when sources share hidden failures, whether additional observations are worth their cost, and eventually whether intelligent agents can notice when their evidence-generation process is unreliable.
