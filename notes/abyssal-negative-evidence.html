What Nothing Tells You

Negative evidence, correlated sensor failure, minimum-KL region assertions, and why an empty satellite pass is still information.

ABYSSAL Series · Part III of VI — Negative Evidence & Constrained Updating All 6 chapters →

In real search operations, positive clues are rare; negative evidence—"we swept this corridor and found nothing"—is the dominant engine that moves the map. Yet negative evidence is deceptively treacherous. Count a miss too weakly and you waste scarce ship-days re-searching cleared ground. Count repeated misses too aggressively and you declare a target absent from the exact patch of seabed or forest where it is silently resting.

This essay unpacks the four mathematical mechanisms ABYSSAL uses to turn empty searches, human judgements, and even blank satellite frames into disciplined belief updates—building directly on the intuition introduced in The Fifth Miss.

1. The miss likelihood and the anatomy of repeated failure

Suppose a search pass covers fraction \(f_i \in [0, 1]\) of cell \(i\) with conditional Probability of Detection \(d \in [0, 1]\) (the probability of spotting the target if it is inside the swept footprint) and analyst reliability \(r \in [0, 1]\). If the pass finds nothing, the survival (miss) likelihood for cell \(i\) is:

$$L_i = 1 - r \cdot d \cdot f_i$$

For a single clean pass, this formula is exact. The trouble begins on the second, third, and fourth passes with the same method. Imagine a side-scan sonar flying over rugged volcanic ridges, or a visual helicopter crew scanning dense pine canopy. If the target lies in an acoustic shadow or beneath a thick hemlock, the first pass misses it for a persistent physical reason. Flying the exact same sensor over the same patch four times does not give you four independent coins; it often gives you one coin flipped once and photocopied three times.

Live Model v5/v6 · Sub-Cell Repeat-Credit Survival

Rather than treating cell-level polygons as coarse binary masks, ABYSSAL evaluates every admitted search at an \(8 \times 8\) sub-cell grid (\(64\) sub-cells per map cell). Within each method group \(g\) (e.g., visual foot teams, scent dogs, side-scan sonar), let \(e_k(s) = r_k d_k \mathbf{1}[s \in F_k]\) be the effective detection of pass \(k\) at sub-cell \(s\), and let \(e_g^{\max}(s) = \max_{k \in g} e_k(s)\). With an analyst-stated repeat-credit parameter \(\rho_g \in [0, 1]\), the sub-cell survival probability interpolates smoothly between max-only (\(\rho_g = 0\), default: repeating an identical pass earns zero extra credit) and fully independent (\(\rho_g = 1\)):

$$S_g(s) = \bigl(1 - e_g^{\max}(s)\bigr)^{1 - \rho_g} \prod_{k \in g} \bigl(1 - e_k(s)\bigr)^{\rho_g}$$

For \(k\) identical passes of effectiveness \(e\) covering the same sub-cell, this reduces cleanly to \(S_g = (1 - e)^{n_{\text{eff}}}\) where the effective number of independent passes is \(n_{\text{eff}} = 1 + \rho(k - 1)\). Distinct method groups (for example, a foot team followed by a scent dog) multiply across groups because their failure modes differ.

Compare that to a separate generative family—a Uniform-Beta shared-detectability model, where the target's unknown local detectability \(D \sim \operatorname{Uniform}(0, 1)\) is fixed across all \(k\) passes. Integrating \(D\) out analytically yields:

$$\mathbb{E}\bigl[(1 - D)^k\bigr] = \int_0^1 (1 - u)^k \, du = \frac{1}{k + 1}$$

Notice the qualitative chasm: exponential decay \((1 - e)^k\) drives survival to \(6.25\%\) after four \(e = 0.5\) passes, whereas power-law shared detectability \(1/(k + 1)\) still leaves 20.0% survival after four passes—and 16.7% after five. 2. A satellite pass is a completed search

One of ABYSSAL's cleanest recent ideas connects open Earth-observation data directly to negative-evidence search accounting: a Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) satellite pass that sees nothing is a completed search.

Sentinel-1 C-band SAR passes over vast stretches of ocean regardless of cloud cover or darkness. Detecting a vessel on SAR depends on target length, wind speed, sea state, and incidence angle. Rather than pretending an uncalibrated satellite frame proves a ship is absent—or throwing away free global coverage altogether—ABYSSAL lets an analyst import a satellite scene footprint GeoJSON and acquisition date, consult an editable target length class × wind speed POD table (with an illustrative Beta concentration \(k = 10\) uncertainty spread), and create a Draft completed search.

As POD shrinks (a small wooden boat in high wind), the pass moves the posterior less; as POD rises (a 200-metre steel tanker in calm seas), an empty pass meaningfully depresses belief inside the swath—while remaining tagged as an analyst assumption until calibrated against planted or known targets.

3. Minimum-KL region assertions: "Not here, 5%"

Sometimes domain knowledge does not arrive as a sensor sweep or a witness bearing. An experienced mountain rescue leader looks at a sheer ice cliff and says: "I don't think it's zero, but there's at most a 5% chance a tired hiker climbed into that basin."

How should a probability map honour a human statement of the form \(P'(R) = q\) over a drawn polygon \(R\) without destroying the fine spatial structure already learned from terrain, witnesses, and prior searches? The principled answer from information geometry is the minimum-KL projection (I-projection), also known as Jeffrey conditioning: find the unique distribution \(P'\) satisfying \(P'(R) = q\) that minimises the Kullback–Leibler divergence \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P' \,\|\, P)\) from the current map \(P\).

Live Model v6 · Jeffrey / Minimum-KL Area Assertion

Let \(p = P(R) = \sum_{i \in R} P(i)\) be the current conditional mass inside polygon \(R\), and let \(q \in [10^{-4}, 1 - 10^{-4}]\) be the human-stated target mass. Minimising \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P' \,\|\, P)\) subject to \(\sum_{i \in R} P'(i) = q\) yields a closed-form piecewise rescaling that preserves every relative probability ratio inside \(R\) and outside \(R\):

$$P'(i) = \begin{cases} P(i)\dfrac{q}{p}, & i \in R \\[8pt] P(i)\dfrac{1 - q}{1 - p}, & i \notin R \end{cases} \qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P' \,\|\, P) = q \log_2 \frac{q}{p} + (1 - q)\log_2 \frac{1 - q}{1 - p} \;\text{ bits}$$

When an analyst draws multiple overlapping region assertions simultaneously, ABYSSAL first verifies joint feasibility via a linear programme (rejecting contradictory constraints instead of oscillating) and then solves for the unique joint I-projection via log-space Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF). Every assertion reports its exact information cost in bits.

4. Entropy, perplexity, and the absence-assurance boundary

To keep analysts honest about how concentrated—or diffuse—the map really is, ABYSSAL reports Shannon entropy \(H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i\) (in bits) and perplexity \(2^H\) (the effective number of equally likely grid cells remaining). Notably, a surprising negative search can legitimately increase entropy: if you were 90% sure the target lay in Room A and a thorough sweep of Room A comes back empty, your belief flattens toward 50/50—raising entropy from \(0.469\) bits to \(1.000\) bit. Honesty requires showing uncertainty growing when reality contradicts a confident prior.

For ecological monitoring, invasive-species surveillance, or site clearance, a separate absence-assurance calculator answers the inverse question: given a prior probability of presence \(\psi\) and conditionally independent visits of fixed detection probability \(p\), how many clean visits \(k\) are needed to push remaining presence below \(\varepsilon\)?

$$P(\text{present} \mid k \text{ clean visits}) = \frac{\psi (1 - p)^k}{(1 - \psi) + \psi (1 - p)^k} \le \varepsilon \;\Longleftrightarrow\; k \ge \frac{\ln\!\left(\dfrac{\psi (1 - \varepsilon)}{(1 - \psi)\varepsilon}\right)}{-\ln(1 - p)}$$

5. The Calibration Gate: Wilson vs. Poisson intervals

Every formula above depends on one critical honesty check: how do you know your POD is really 0.70 and not 0.30? ABYSSAL enforces a hard architectural Calibration Gate. An uncalibrated automated detector can generate contacts (Context only); it is barred from creating admissible observations that alter the belief map. To cross the gate, a detector must carry held-out labelled evaluation samples separated strictly by origin (synthetic injected targets can never masquerade as real field data), with detection rates bounded by Wilson score intervals and spatial false-alarm rates per km² bounded by Poisson (chi-square) count intervals rather than mislabelled binomial bounds.

Once your belief map honestly tracks both positive sightings and negative searches, a practical economic question emerges: given a finite budget of ship-hours, fuel, and crew, where should you look next—and when is the rational move to stop looking altogether? That is Part IV.
